## 1. Structure of DP assessment

**Documented (IB official material):**

- The IB describes DP assessment as *criterion-related*: "IB Diploma Programme assessment is
  criterion-related, rather than measured against the performance of other exam takers" (IB,
  *Assessment guide for teachers and coordinators*). An IB-derived mathematics guide states that
  "the approach to assessment used by the IB is criterion-related, not norm-referenced"
  (peda.net, quoting the IB subject guide). The 2004 policy text is cited as *Diploma Programme
  assessment: Principles and practice* (2004, updated 2010).
- The assessment cycle is listed by the IB as: exam preparation; assessments undertaken by
  candidates; standard setting; marking; grade award; "at risk" re-marking; release of results;
  enquiry upon results; assessment design (IB, *Assessment guide for teachers and coordinators*).
- Examiners: "The IB only appoints examiners who are subject experts and have teaching experience.
  The majority of examiners are experienced IB teachers" (IB, *IB student assessment* web page).
  Examiners pass three stages — practice, qualification and live marking — and "live marking is
  monitored using seeds" (IB, teachers' guide). The IB's guide for chief and principal examiners
  says seeding is the quality method for e-marked components: examiners mark responses already
  marked by the principal examiner and senior team, and their marks are compared against those
  reference marks; moderation is used for components not marked on screen (IB, *Assessment guide
  for chief and principal examiners*; *Assessment guide for examiners*).
- On-screen marking: the IB's students-and-parents guide defines e-marking as "the process used
  when your work is marked on-screen by examiners". IB assessment-forum presentations by Richard
  Penrose (2013, 2015) describe candidate data being sent to RM for upload into the marking
  system, under-performing examiners being "coached or removed from the session", and
  standardisation and grade-award meetings being held remotely.
- Internal assessment (IA): teachers mark against IB criteria; a sample is sent to the IB for
  external moderation; "If the school's marking standard differs from the IB standard, an
  adjustment may be applied to the marks for that component across the relevant group" (third-party
  summaries: RevisionDojo, SaveMyExams, PlusPlusTutors). The IB's chief-examiner guide adds that
  "The grade boundaries for internally assessed and non-examination (coursework) components are
  set at the beginning of the course and are not revised each session because the task does not
  change."
- Diploma threshold: "Students need 24 points to be awarded a Diploma" (UCAS Expert Group report;
  RevisionDojo) and "a total of 24 points is necessary for the diploma, but it does not guarantee
  the award" (RevisionDojo), i.e. additional pass conditions apply.

**Share of marks that is external vs internal — partly documented (third-party only, conflicting):**
sources give "IAs typically make up 20-25% of the final grade", "external assessment at 70–80% of most
subject grades", and "Internal Assessment can account for 20-60% of the final DP mark, depending on
the subject", with arts/design subjects weighting IA more heavily. The authoritative figure is in each
subject guide (to fetch).

**UNVERIFIED (model memory), well-known structure:** six subjects (three or four at Higher Level,
rest at Standard Level) each graded 1–7 (max 42); Theory of Knowledge and Extended Essay combine in a
matrix for up to 3 core points; CAS must be completed but carries no points; total 45. Failing
conditions include: fewer than 24 points, an "N" or grade E in TOK/EE, a grade 1 in any subject, more
than two grade 2s, more than three grade 3s, fewer than 12 points at HL (three HL) and fewer than 9
points at SL. Examination papers are set centrally and marked by external examiners who are not the
candidate's teachers; the examination sits in May (northern hemisphere) and November.

## 2. How grade boundaries are set and how standards are maintained

**Documented:**

- IB official page: "The grade boundaries are agreed at a meeting of the senior examiners and IB
  staff shortly before the issue of results. The grade boundaries are determined using a
  combination of judgment, statistical evidence, grade descriptors and feedback from teachers."
  And: "The principal means of setting judgmentally determined grade boundaries is by a review of
  the quality of candidate work against grade descriptors." "Grade standards are documented and
  exemplified, and judgments made about grade boundaries are checked by a number of statistical
  indicators" (IB, *IB student assessment*).
- Standards over time: the IB's examiner guide states that "marks may vary between sessions as a
  result of the different questions, but the grade standard from previous sessions must be
  carried forward", and that a chief examiner is responsible for ensuring that standards are
  maintained from one year to the next (IB, *Assessment guide for chief and principal examiners* /
  *examiners*).
- Statistically Recommended Boundaries (SRBs): the IB research page *Statistical grade boundary
  setting approaches* describes SRBs as a "statistical estimate of where the grade boundaries
  should be to maintain standards from one year to the next", comparing the prior cohort's results
  with the current session's data, after which awarders use examiner judgement and script evidence
  to finalise boundaries. The IB describes its historical model as "weak criterion referencing".
- Independent review of the IB's statistical method: AlphaPlus produced for the IB a *Literature
  review* (December 2021), a *Simulation analysis* (September 2022) and an *Executive summary of
  the SRB research study* (October 2022). The executive summary notes that the approach "is
  still superior to many other approaches as it aims to account for any change in cohort ability,
  though it is weaker than using prior attainment to maintain outcomes", that "only around half of
  IB's contexts meet this assumption" (the comparability assumption behind the method), that
  SRBs "work less well" with very small candidate numbers or when a new curriculum/assessment
  model is introduced, and that "there are some grade award contexts where alternative methods
  might be more suitable". Item response theory is mentioned as one of the approaches covered.
- IRT/Rasch: Badham, Meadows & Baird (Frontiers in Education, August 2025) apply a Rasch Partial
  Credit Model to historical IB assessment data to estimate relative "difficulty" of IB subjects and
  languages; they conclude that post-hoc modelling has limits. This is research, not the operational
  awarding method.
- Policy documents: *Assessment principles and practices — Quality assessments in a digital age*
  exists in a 2018 English edition; the Spanish edition states the original was "published in
  July 2019 and updated in November 2021"; a current IB PDF lists "Published May 2026". There are
  derived guides for students/parents, teachers/coordinators, examiners, and chief/principal
  examiners (URLs in TO-FETCH.md).
- Organisation: the IB Assessment Centre in Cardiff, Wales, is "home to over 200 members of the
  IB's 700 staff" and "is responsible for creating and managing assessment activities for the IB's
  four programmes" (IB news release). A Cardiff job advert for "Manager, Assessment Research and
  Design" (closed February 2020) places the team within the "Assessment Principles and Practice
  team (APP)", leading research that "analyses the effectiveness of IB's assessment content and
  procedures" and supporting colleagues "so that assessment design is valid, reliable and feasible".
  The search results do not use the word "psychometrician" for any IB team.

**Pandemic (documented):**

- May 2020: exams cancelled; the IB awarded calculated grades based on coursework, predicted
  grades and historical data (IB *Assessment model letter May 2020*; the IB said it would set
  boundaries "rather than tightly following the grade descriptors"). After review requests from
  about 700 of 3,020 schools, the IB adjusted some results (Gulf News; Times Higher Education; IB
  update M20).
- November 2020: "grade boundaries were set separately for each route" (exam / non-exam) (IB news).
- May 2021: dual route; mean diploma score rose from 31.34 (M20) to 33.02 (M21) and pass rate from
  85.18% to 88.96% (IB figures quoted by schoolscompared.com).
- May 2022: adapted model with fewer components; the IB said it would "adjust boundaries, building
  on its 2021 approach" (IB M22 letter).
- May 2023: "The IB intends to set the distribution of awarded grades to the pre-pandemic levels of
  May 2019", using all components, "in line with other awarding bodies" (IB, *Further update for
  the May 2023 examination session*). M23 mean total 30.24 (IB final bulletin May 2023; School
  Management Plus), M24 pass rate 80.5%, M25 pass rate 81.9%, mean total 30.6, mean grade 4.7 (IB
  statistical bulletins).

**Interpretation:** the IB's own documents and the AlphaPlus review show a *comparable-outcomes-style*
system: an explicit statistical anchor to the previous session's grade distribution, adjusted by
examiner judgement against grade descriptors. The pandemic episode shows the same mechanism used
deliberately to lift, then return, the distribution — i.e. the IB publicly manages the distribution
of awarded grades. This supports the op-ed's claim that a given IB grade is intended to mean the same
thing over time, but it also means the IB standard is *not* purely criterion-referenced (the IB itself
says "weak criterion referencing").

## 3. The ACER / ISA question

**Documented:**

- ACER's International Schools' Assessment (ISA): developed by ACER; "based on the assessment
  frameworks of the OECD's Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)"; covers reading,
  mathematical literacy, narrative writing, expository writing and scientific literacy for grades
  3 to 10; used by international schools "to monitor student performance over time"; participating
  schools "follow a diverse range of curricula including International Baccalaureate (IB), British
  National, USA and more" (acer.org ISA pages).
- IB–ACER relationship: "In 2009, the IB contracted the Australian Council for Educational Research
  (ACER) to undertake an analysis of the International Schools' Assessment (ISA) data for
  participating IB schools" (IB ISA final report, 2010). ACER repeated this for the IB in 2021
  ("report prepared for the International Baccalaureate by ACER in February 2021") and 2026 (ISA
  2023–24 and PISA 2022 data). These studies compare PYP/MYP students with non-IB students — they
  concern the Primary and Middle Years Programmes, not the DP.
- Other ACER work commissioned by the IB: Edwards & Underwood, *IB graduates in Australian
  universities: entry and outcomes* (ACER for IBO, September 2012); ACER, *The relationship between
  teaching, learning and digital assessment* (full report for the IB, 2019).
- PISA leadership: ACER led the PISA consortium from the late 1990s through PISA 2000, 2003, 2006,
  2009 and 2012 (ACER history; Turner & Adams 2010 describe 1998–2010; Wikipedia says "1997 until
  2015"; partners included Cito, ETS and NIER). For PISA 2018 "overall co-ordination ... the
  development of instruments, and scaling and analysis were managed by Claudia Tamassia at ETS";
  for PISA 2022 "ETS in the United States was the Core A contractor". For PISA 2025 ACER returned
  as "Core 1 contractor for overall management". The exact year ETS took over (2015) was **not
  confirmed** by a search result (**UNVERIFIED (model memory)**: ETS led the PISA 2015 core
  consortium).
- The DP assessment is designed in-house: the Cardiff IB Assessment Centre "is responsible for
  creating and managing assessment activities for the IB's four programmes" (IB news). External
  methodological work the IB cites comes from AlphaPlus (SRB review 2021–22), Ecctis/UK NARIC
  (alignment and benchmarking) and Oxford University researchers (Rasch study 2025), not ACER.

**Conclusion on the user's belief:** The IB and ACER do have a documented relationship, but it is a
*client–contractor research relationship* (ACER analyses ISA/PISA data and university outcomes for
the IB). There is **no evidence** in the search results that ACER designed, or advises on, the DP's
examination and grade-award system. The ISA is ACER's own PISA-style monitoring test for grades 3–10,
not part of DP assessment. The op-ed should therefore not say that "the organisation behind ISA
designed IB assessment"; it can say that the IB's assessment is designed by a dedicated assessment
centre in Cardiff with a research-and-design unit, that its statistical awarding method has been
independently reviewed (AlphaPlus), and that the IB commissions outcomes research from ACER,
Ecctis/NARIC and HESA.

## 4. Evidence on stability and comparability

**IB grade-distribution data (documented):** Mean total diploma points, IB bulletins: M2019 ≈ 29.65–
29.7 (provisional table), M2020 31.34, M2021 33.02 (or 32.97 in another table), M2022 31.98/32.00,
M2023 30.24, M2025 30.6; pass rates ≈ 80.0% (M23), 80.5% (M24), 81.9% (M25), versus 85–89% in the
pandemic years. The IB publishes both provisional and final bulletins and figures differ slightly
between them. The post-2023 figures are close to the 2019 level, consistent with the IB's stated
intention to return to the 2019 distribution. (A third-party site reports M2026 mean 30.8 and pass
rate 82.6% — not confirmed against an IB document.)

**UK — comparability with A levels (partly documented):**
- UCAS Expert Group report (2006) on admitting the IB Diploma to the Tariff describes the original
  comparability study.
- Green & Vignoles (2012), Cambridge Assessment: using later university performance, the UCAS tariff
  "overvalued both the IB and vocational BTECs" (Times Higher Education; Cambridge Assessment PDF).
  This is the main *independent* UK study and it points in the *opposite* direction from the op-ed's
  thesis for the UK tariff — it should be acknowledged.
- A "UK NARIC 2016 IB vs A-level benchmarking study" was **not found**; Ecctis (ex-UK NARIC) has done
  for the IB: the Norway alignment study (2019, report dated Sept 2018), a CEFR mapping of DP
  languages (Aug 2023), DP country alignment studies (Victoria, Ontario, Finland, Singapore, South
  Korea, US; 2023–2025) and a DP-vs-GCSE mathematics grade comparison (2025). Ecctis also benchmarked
  South Africa's NSC against the IB Diploma and Cambridge A levels for Umalusi (2022). The
  "IB vs A-level standard comparison 2016/2023" named in the brief remains **unverified**.

**Australia — ATAR conversion precedent (documented):**
- Until 2023 results, ACTAC used "a direct conversion from whole IB Diploma results to an ATAR
  equivalent": 45 → 99.95, 44 → 99.75, 43 → 99.45 (VTAC 2021 table). Earlier, the national
  conversion was a weighted average of the NSW, Victoria and SA methods (QTAC paper).
- Criticism: the conversions were seen as "overly generous, with all IB scores of 45 equating to a
  perfect ATAR of 99.95, while all IB scores above 33 translated to ATARs above 90" (KIS Academics).
- Reform: ACTAC introduced a "finer grained conversion" "based on more detailed percentage marks for
  each subject" for students starting the DP in 2021 (results 2023, admissions 2024); "not all IB
  45s would convert to a 99.95". QTAC estimated ~60% of students unchanged, 25% up, 15% down. The
  scheme is now called the "ACTAC calculated IB Admissions Score (IBAS)" (IB Australasia; QTAC 2022
  fact sheet). **Method lesson:** Australia moved to *more* resolution inside the IB score (using
  subject percentage marks supplied by the IB) rather than compressing the top of the scale.

**Sweden (documented):** UHR cut the maximum IB meritvärde from 45 to 44 points = 20.00 in 2016 (press
note 2016-11-28); Riksdag motions say IB applications fell afterwards; UHR's 2022 impact assessment
conceded that IB (and European Baccalaureate) students "får en felaktig meritvärdering och
platsfördelning"; a table in force from 30 June 2025 gives 38 → 19.38, 40 → 19.70, 42 → 19.90.

**Denmark (partly documented):** parents' submissions to the Folketing (2024) say an IB 6 converts to
10.8 on the 7-step scale and disadvantages IB applicants in kvote 1; Danes Worldwide obtained a
commitment to annual review of the conversion (2023); the Danish Ministry (UVM) commissioned an
Epinion survey on IB in 2025 (PDF link in TO-FETCH). No 2022–23 Danish rule change was confirmed.

**Norway (documented):**
- HK-dir presented a new IB conversion table in December 2022 (NIBS letter, May 2023). HK-dir's
  statistic: "0,08 % av søkerne med norsk videregående opplæring (studieforberedende) fikk snitt 6,0
  i opptaket, mens 2,8 % av IB-søkerne fikk dette" — the government's stated reason that IB
  applicants have an advantage (regjeringen.no).
- HK-dir drafted a table on a mandate from the Ministry, "sent på høyring frå departementet i 2024",
  using "data frå IB-stiftinga med poeng frå diplomresultata hos alle IB-skular i verda"; a version
  was on hearing "vinteren 2025"; the current proposal (hearing opened autumn 2026, deadline
  10.12.2026) would start at 20 IB points, use only data from IB lines in Norway, and apply from
  admission to 2030–31. HK-dir's own "framlegg" of 26.10.2026 argued for global IB statistics
  because a Norway-only sample is "langt mindre". The method: "HK-dir har om lag hundre
  omrekningstabellar ... og nyttar ein statistisk metode utarbeidd av Norsk Reknesentral (NR)";
  "Den nye tabellen er basert på offisiell karakterstatistikk frå International Baccalaureate
  Organization (IB). Den statistiske metoden er vurdert av Norsk Regnesentral."
- Stortinget: Dok. 8:76 S (2024–2025) asked the government to wait "til tilstrekkelig involvering av
  berørte parter er ivaretatt" and argued the table makes Norway "betydelig strengere ... enn de
  øvrige nordiske landene". NIBS: the table "forringer verdien av IB Diploma sammenlignet med norsk
  vitnemål".
- A Legeforeningen (Norwegian Medical Association) attachment "vedlegg omrekningstabell for IB"
  exists on legeforeningen.no, implying a Medical Association hearing response — content not
  retrieved.

## 5. Predictive validity

**UK (documented, all IB-commissioned, HESA data):**
- HESA (2011): IB Diploma students vs A level/Scottish Higher holders; IB students "outperformed in
  most subject areas for class of degree"; continuation 94.7% vs 94.2% (n.s.).
- HESA / IB *UK postsecondary outcomes* (2016): IB students "significantly more likely to attend a
  top 20 HEI, to graduate with honours and to engage in further study".
- Duxbury et al. (2021), *UK higher education outcomes*: after controlling for academic ability, IB
  Diploma holders "3.02 times more likely" than A-level holders to obtain a first-class degree and
  "40% more likely ... to obtain at least an upper second-class honours degree".
- Counter-evidence: Green & Vignoles (2012) found the UCAS tariff overvalues the IB relative to
  later university performance (see §4).

**Australia (documented):** Edwards & Underwood (ACER for IBO, 2012), 154 DP graduates at two
universities: "when student characteristics and secondary school achievement are taken into
account, IB students and non-IB students perform at similar levels"; "the correlations between
secondary school grades and performance at university were higher for IB DP graduates" (a direct
predictive-validity finding); IB graduates more likely to complete within five years.

**USA (documented, IB-linked):** Caspary & Bland (2011), 4,845 Florida students: "average IB exam
score was the strongest predictor of college GPA"; Shah, Dean & Chen (2010, University of
California): IB exam scores the best predictor of college GPA; Bergeron (2015), National Student
Clearinghouse tracking. A literature review notes that "almost all of the research currently
available on college admission and success for IB students is based on research studies commissioned
and sponsored by the International Baccalaureate Organization".

**Asia-Pacific (documented, IB summary):** at one of three universities the DP score was a
significant predictor of GPA; at another no significant GPA difference between DP and non-DP.

**Norway (gap):** UK NARIC (2019) *DP alignment with Norwegian upper secondary education* concluded
"IB World Schools in Norway are well equipped to deliver the DP in a way that is compatible with the
Norwegian system" — a curriculum/outcomes alignment study, not a performance study. **No Norwegian
study of IB graduates' university performance (medicine or otherwise) was found**; HK-dir's only
published figure is the share of applicants at 6.0. This is the single most important empirical gap
for the op-ed: the authorities infer "advantage" from the *distribution of converted points*, not
from *subsequent performance*.

## 6. Contrast with Norwegian grading (brief)

**Documented:**
- Standpunkt grades are set by the subject teacher: "faglæreren skal bruke sitt faglige skjønn til
  å gjøre en samlet vurdering av elevens kompetanse ut fra kompetansemålene i læreplanen for faget",
  within forskrift til opplæringslova kap. 3 and Udir's rundskriv on individual assessment
  (UDIR-2-2020); the competence shown during the year ("underveisvurdering") is part of the basis
  (Statsforvalteren guidance documents). The grade is an individual decision that can be appealed
  (10 days) — i.e. anchored in law and procedure, not in any external measurement.
- Exams: the draw ("trekk") is regulated by Udir-2-2018 / forskrift § 3-26, now opplæringsforskriften
  (2024) § 9-30 "talet på eksamenar"; county authorities run the draw. (Exact number of exams per
  Vg3 student not captured — **UNVERIFIED (model memory)**: a Vg3 student on a study-preparatory
  programme normally has a compulsory written Norwegian exam plus draws, in total about 4 exams
  across Vg2–Vg3, so most subject grades on the vitnemål are standpunkt grades only.)
- Pandemic: all exams cancelled spring 2020 (written and oral); written exams cancelled 2021 (oral
  held for final-year students; the regulation's usual requirement for setting standpunkt grades was
  set aside); all exams cancelled spring 2022 with some exceptions. Udir states that averages for
  2019–20 to 2021–22 "are not directly comparable with other years". Lillebø, Skålholt & Kindt
  (Tidsskrift for samfunnsforskning, 2024) find that weak students completed more easily and that
  "snittkarakterene økte", questioning whether the cohorts were actually study-prepared.
- Grade inflation / standpunkt–exam gap: Hovdhaugen, Prøitz & Seland (Acta Didactica Norge, 2018)
  on exam vs standpunkt grades; SSB on larger standpunkt–exam gaps for immigrant pupils; NIFU for
  Udir comparing exam/standpunkt grades in public vs private upper secondary schools; a KD-
  commissioned research project on "kvalitet, karakterinflasjon og segregering" in public/private
  upper secondary schools is in progress (Statsforvalteren Innlandet slides, Sept 2026). Udir
  grade-trend series were not retrieved (search budget) — see TO-FETCH.

## 8. Implications for the conversion-table debate

1. **Asymmetry of measurement.** The IB grade is produced by a process with an explicit standards-
   maintenance mechanism (previous-session statistical anchor + examiner judgement against published
   descriptors + external marking + moderation). Norwegian standpunkt grades have no equivalent
   external anchor, and the exam component that could act as one was removed entirely in 2020 and
   2022 and partly in 2021. A statistical mapping that treats the Norwegian distribution as the fixed
   reference therefore maps a (relatively) stable scale onto a drifting one.
2. **The "2.8% vs 0.08% at 6.0" argument is a distribution argument, not a validity argument.** Nothing
   in the material shows that IB applicants converted to 6.0 under-perform at university; the only
   validity evidence (UK, Australia, US) says IB scores predict university performance at least as
   well as national grades, with the Australian ACER study showing *higher* correlation with university
   outcomes for IB graduates. The honest caveat: nearly all of this research is IB-commissioned, and
   the independent UK study (Green & Vignoles 2012) found the UCAS tariff overvalued the IB.
3. **Method precedent.** Australia responded to "too generous" by increasing resolution (using subject
   percentage marks within each IB total), not by flattening the top. Norway could ask the IB for the
   same percentage-mark data. Sweden's 2016 cut (45 → 44 for max) is a cautionary tale: IB enrolment
   fell and UHR later conceded the valuation was "felaktig".
4. **Selection effects must be separated from scale effects.** A higher share of top scores among IB
   applicants may reflect who chooses IB (a small, self-selected group) rather than lenient scoring;
   the IB global mean is ~30/45 and only a few percent score 40+. The authorities' own reasoning (global
   vs Norway-only data; HK-dir vs Ministry disagree) shows the result depends heavily on the reference
   population chosen — a sign the method is not measuring a property of the IB scale.
5. **What to say about ACER.** Do not claim ACER designed IB assessment. Say instead: the IB runs its
   own assessment centre (Cardiff) with a research-and-design unit; its awarding statistics have been
   independently reviewed (AlphaPlus 2021–22); its outcomes have been studied by ACER (Australia), HESA
   (UK) and UK NARIC/Ecctis (alignment, including with Norway in 2019).
6. **Weak points to pre-empt.** (a) The IB itself calls its model "weak criterion referencing" and uses
   statistics to hold the *distribution* roughly constant, so "a 7 always means the same" is a design
   intention checked statistically, not a psychometric guarantee; (b) the IB visibly inflated grades in
   2020–22 before correcting; (c) ~20–25% of marks are teacher-marked IA (moderated).
