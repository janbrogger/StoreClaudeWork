# Secondary-school grade inflation outside Norway: what the international record shows

Research note prepared 2026-11-02 for an op-ed on Norway's proposed IB-to-Norwegian-grade-points conversion table.
Scope: evidence that school-awarded grades in Western countries have risen without a matching rise in externally
measured achievement, and how those countries police the gap. Norway is covered in a separate note.

Method and caveats. Only WebSearch snippets and GitHub-hosted files were reachable; every ministry, statistics-agency,
journal and newspaper site was blocked, and the shared search budget ran out before sections 6-7 were fully covered.
Every figure below traces to a snippet or a downloaded file (see `top_grade_shares.csv`, `studies.csv` and the Sources
list). Statements from model memory are marked **UNVERIFIED (model memory)** and are not in the CSVs. `TO-FETCH.md`
lists the primary documents to pull on an unrestricted machine; the most important are the JCQ/Ofqual results archives,
the KMK Abiturnoten tables, Skolverket's statistics database and the Swedish Henrekson inquiry (2025).

Chart-ready series are in `top_grade_shares.csv` (94 rows). The strongest for a chart are: UK A-level A*/A share
2019-2026 (with the 2020-2021 teacher-assessment spike and the 2023 reset), UK GCSE 7/A+ 2019-2025, US mean GPA
1990-2021 from three independent data sets set against NAEP long-term-trend scores, Germany's share of 1,0 Abitur
2020-2025 and share of 1,0-1,9 (2006 vs 2024), Denmark's STX/HHX/HTX averages 2010-2022, and Ireland's H1 share 2024-2026.

## 1. Sweden

**The research literature is the oldest and most explicit in Europe, and it is about teacher-set grades drifting from
national tests.**

- Wikström & Wikström (2005, *Economics of Education Review* 24(3): 309-322) built a grade-inflation measure (grades
  relative to an external standard) and a competition measure for upper-secondary schools. Their conclusion, per the
  IDEAS/GUPEA abstracts: competition does not reliably explain grade inflation, but "large gaps in grade inflation
  appear between public and independent schools and between for-profit and non-profit schools".
- IFAU 2010:23 (Tyrefors Hinnerich et al.): when national-test essays were re-graded blind, "original teachers gave on
  average 13 percent higher grades than the re-grading teachers".
- Hansson, Henrekson & Vlachos (IFN 2011, *Godtyckliga betyg skadar skolan*): at three schools more than half of the
  pupils received a higher course grade than their national-test grade; they argue the course-based gymnasium system
  (final grade after every course) makes inflation worse than in compulsory school.
- Vlachos analysed c. 32,000 national tests that Skolinspektionen had re-graded externally: independent schools gave
  higher internal grades than the external re-graders did, relative to municipal schools (Sveriges Radio). Tyrefors
  Hinnerich & Vlachos (2017; press coverage SVT "Friskolor rättar snällare än kommunala skolor") found that free schools
  both grade the national tests more generously and set course grades more generously relative to those tests; Vlachos
  2018: "Jo, friskolorna har visst betygsinflation". (Journal details of the 2017 paper: UNVERIFIED (model memory) -
  *Labour Economics* 47.)
- Riksrevisionen RiR 2022:22 (*Statens insatser för likvärdig betygssättning*): "det fortfarande finns stora skillnader
  mellan betyg och resultat på nationella prov"; the 2011 rule that national-test results be given "särskild vikt" "har
  haft liten påverkan på likvärdigheten i betygssättningen". The gaps are largest for pupils who failed the test, so a
  mechanical link to tests "riskerar att öka utslagningen".
- Policy response: in June 2023 the government appointed Magnus Henrekson to lead the inquiry *Likvärdiga betyg och
  meritvärden* (dir. 2023:95), explicitly "för att stoppa betygsinflationen", reporting by 21 Feb 2025. The inquiry's
  proposals and any 2025-2026 legislation ("nationella prov ska styra betygen") were **not retrieved** (search budget);
  see TO-FETCH. Current law: where a national test exists, the result "ska särskilt beaktas" when grading.
- Series: Skolverket's year-9 meritvärde rose steadily to 209.6 in 2008/09 (16-subject scale, max 320) and, on the
  17-subject scale (max 340), peaked in 2021 at the highest level since 1998, falling 2.9 points to 229.2 in 2022
  (2021 therefore c. 232.1, derived). At the same time 16.3 % of the 2024 cohort (c. 19,900 pupils) left year 9
  ineligible for national gymnasium programmes - rising top grades alongside a rising share failing. Gymnasium mean
  grade points on vocational programmes were 13.3 in both 2022 and 2023; the national series 2014-2023 is in
  Skolverket's 2023 report appendix (not captured in snippets).
- IB conversion in Sweden: UHR's table in force from 30 June 2025 maps IB total points to a meritvärde of 10.00-20.00.
  It is contested: a 2024 UHR consequence analysis and Svenskar i Världen's consultation response, a 2017 written
  question in the Riksdag ("EB- och IB-elevernas betyg"), and a new motion 2026/27:53 (Louise Meijer, M) "Omvandling av
  IB-elevers poäng till meritvärde". A motion claims UHR compares IB pupils with natural-science-programme pupils, and
  that applications to IB fell after UHR's adjustment (claim, not verified statistic).

**Why it matters for the op-ed:** Sweden is the clearest case of a Nordic system where the reference distribution
(school grades) is known - by its own audit office - to be inflated relative to the external test, and where the remedy
being legislated is to tie grades to the external test, not to re-scale external qualifications to the grades.

## 2. Denmark

- Averages have risen monotonically. Danmarks Statistik (2018): "karaktergennemsnittet stiger for alle grupper af
  studenter"; largest rise on HTX (+0.9, 2010-2018). UVM/STIL (2018 table note): STX exam result incl. bonus A rose
  from 6.9 (2010) to 7.4 (2017), HHX from 6.1 to 6.8; HTX 7.5 in 2017. DST (June 2022): STX and HTX averaged 7.7 in
  2022, "rekordhøjt"; the trend "er kun gået én vej siden 2010, og det er opad". 2023-2025 figures not captured
  (uddannelsesstatistik.dk, see TO-FETCH).
- Teacher-given year grades (årskarakterer) exceed exam grades: KL's note on STX found written exam grades on average
  0.8 grade points below written year grades; CEPOS: "tendensen til at give for høje årskarakterer bliver mere udtalt";
  EVA has a dedicated note on årskarakterer vs prøvekarakterer on STX (numbers not in snippets).
- 2025 supervision: STUK's screening of 2023 and 2024 grade data found that institutions have "stor karakterdiskrepans"
  especially in mathematics, and that the national gap between mean year grades and mean exam grades is larger for
  women than for men; the ministry (Aug 2025) answered that all pupils must get the grade they are entitled to,
  "hverken højere eller lavere".
- EVA (2016, *Karaktergivning i gymnasiet*) and a Folketing answer note that in Danish and English the grade
  distribution has moved towards the expected one over time, while for other subjects it is less clear, raising the
  question of "karakterinflation" in the period.
- IB conversion in Denmark: the table used from the 2018 intake starts at 18 IB points = 2.4, 24 = 4.4, 28 = 6.0 on the
  7-point scale (full table in uvm.dk 170629 note). Guiding principle: "ansøgeres mulighed for at få en studieplads ...
  skal ikke påvirkes af, om adgangsgrundlaget er dansk, udenlandsk eller internationalt". The Danish IB schools'
  association calls the conversion erroneous and proposed two alternative models; the ministry's position is that the
  table is built on international IB statistics, so Danish IB schools get a comparatively worse conversion than if only
  Danish IB results were used. A 2024 Folketing submission from a parent of a 2023 Danish IB graduate shows the debate
  is live; any 2024-2025 revision was not retrieved.

## 3. United Kingdom

**The UK is the clearest natural experiment: exam-based grades were replaced by teacher assessment for two years and
the top-grade share nearly doubled, then was deliberately deflated back.**

A-level share of A*/A (UK-wide JCQ unless noted):

| Year | A*/A % | Regime |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 25.4 (England 25.2) | exams, comparable outcomes |
| 2020 | 38.1 (England) | centre-assessed grades after the U-turn; the withdrawn algorithm gave 27.6; A* alone jumped from 7.7 % to 14.3 % |
| 2021 | 44.8 (England 44.3) | teacher-assessed grades |
| 2022 | 36.4 | transition, midway |
| 2023 | 27.2 | return to 2019 standards |
| 2024 | 27.8 | |
| 2025 | 28.3 | (secondary source citing JCQ) |
| 2026 | 28.5 | (secondary source citing JCQ) |

GCSE share of grade 7/A and above (UK): 20.8 (2019), 26.2 (2020), 28.9 (2021), 26.3 (2022), 22.0 (2023), 21.8 (2024),
21.9 (2025).

Longer run: the share of A-level papers awarded grade A rose from 8.8 % in 1980 to 17.6 % in 2000 (TES). After A* was
introduced in 2010 the A* share in England stayed flat at 7.7-8.6 % through 2019 (8.1 % 2010, 7.7 % 2013, 8.6 % 2014,
8.0 % 2018, 7.7 % 2019) - the signature of comparable outcomes. **UNVERIFIED (model memory):** the A-grade share
continued rising to c. 27 % (A*/A) by 2010 before the policy froze it, so the UK series is "steady inflation 1982-2010,
plateau 2010-2019, spike 2020-21, reset 2023".

Ofqual's "comparable outcomes": awarding bodies build reference matrices (KS2 -> GCSE; GCSE -> A level) from a base
year, predict each cohort's grade distribution from its prior attainment and set boundaries so that "a pupil gets the
same result this year that they would have gotten last year" (HEPI). First applied to new A levels in 2010 and new GCSEs
in 2011 (inter-board comparability reports; Cambridge Assessment). Individual grades still depend only on marks; the
method fixes where the boundaries fall. Cambridge Assessment (*Comparable Outcomes: Scourge or Scapegoat?*) notes it
"can prevent grade inflation" and asks whether that is always desirable. This is the standard remedy: anchor the
distribution to prior attainment, not to this year's teacher judgements.

IB vs A level benchmarking: UCAS's Expert Group (from 2004) benchmarked IB subjects against A levels and allotted
720 tariff points to a 45-point diploma, "roughly six A grades"; the whole-diploma tariff was replaced by per-subject
points c. 2017. Cambridge Assessment (2016, *Assessing the equivalencies of the UCAS tariff*) found IB and BTEC
"overvalued" relative to A levels when judged by later university achievement. UK NARIC/Ecctis benchmarking of the IB
Diploma against A levels was not found in snippets - **UNVERIFIED (model memory):** UK NARIC (2016) concluded IB HL 7 sits
at or above A* and the Diploma's overall demand exceeds three A levels; retrieve before citing.

## 4. United States

**Three independent data sets show grades rising while every external yardstick (SAT, ACT, NAEP) is flat or falling.**

- NCES High School Transcript Study: mean GPA of graduates 2.68 (1990), 2.94 (2000, Chalkbeat citing NCES), 3.00 (2009),
  3.11 (2019); Chalkbeat's headline: "High school grades are up, but test scores aren't".
- Hurwitz & Lee (2018, College Board / JHU Press *Measuring Success*): among SAT takers 1998-2016, mean GPA 3.27 -> 3.38;
  share with A-range GPA 39 % -> 47 %; mean SAT 1026 -> 1002 (1600 scale). Private schools inflated at about three times
  the rate of public schools; mean GPA 3.56 in the top-decile-inflation schools vs 3.14 in the bottom decile. Critics
  note the College Board affiliation and the 2016 SAT redesign.
- ACT (Sanchez & Moore 2022, *Grade Inflation Continues to Grow in the Past Decade*): 4.3 million ACT-tested students
  in 4,700 public high schools; mean GPA 3.17 (2010) -> 3.36 (2021), robust to student and school controls; "the greatest
  grade inflation occurred between 2018 and 2021", highest in maths, and faster in schools with 75 %+ white/Asian
  enrolment. ACT's 2026 follow-up (R2514) re-uses the series and studies "academically discrepant" students (high GPA,
  low ACT).
- NAEP long-term trend: 13-year-olds "were generally making progress ... until 2012. Since then, scores have trended
  downward" (NAGB); 2012-2020 reading -3, maths -5 (first decline in LTT history); 2020-2023 reading -4, maths -7 (NCES
  as reported) or -9 (other reports); the lowest performers' maths scores are back at 1970s levels. Age-9 series from the
  NCES tables (GitHub mirror): reading 221 (2012) -> 220 (2020) -> 215 (2022); maths 244 (2012) -> 241 (2020) -> 234
  (2022).
- **UNVERIFIED (model memory):** Gershenson (Fordham 2018) matched North Carolina course grades to end-of-course Algebra I
  scores 2005-2016 and found rising grades with no rise in exam scores, with more inflation in affluent schools.

## 5. Germany

- Share of 1,0 Abitur (Profiling Institut compilation of KMK data): c. 2.2 % (2020), 3.4 % (2021, peak), 3.1 % (2023),
  3.1 % (2024), 3.1 % (2025; 8,255 students). dpa, on a different base, gives 2.9 % (2020) and "more than four percent"
  in each year 2021-2024; hessenschau: "gut 9.000" 1,0-Abiture in 2024 among ">370,000" with Hochschulreife.
- Share of grades 1,0-1,9: 20.1 % (2006) -> 30.7 % (2024) (Statista from KMK). National mean 2.35 in 2025 (weighted
  Länder mean); the best averages were 2021 and 2022, "seitdem steigen sie leicht". KMK itself describes a "Peak" in
  2021/2022 and lower values in 2023/2024.
- State series (Schleswig-Holstein Landtag): 2.52 (2010/11), 2.52 (2011/12), 2.54 (2012/13), 2.54 (2013/14); Saxony
  2.29 (2016) improving to 2.24. **UNVERIFIED (model memory):** the KMK national mean improved from about 2.56 (2006)
  to about 2.37 (2019) and c. 2.30 in 2021/22 - retrieve the KMK "Abiturnoten" tables.
- Debate: the Deutscher Lehrerverband speaks of a "massive Entwertung des Abiturs" and an "Einser-Abi-Flut"; news4teachers
  (Feb 2023): "Zahl der Einser-Abiture ist in der Corona-Hochphase deutlich gestiegen"; (July 2026) "Abiturienten
  schneiden erneut besser ab". Note the counter-point: dpa finds "keine eindeutige Tendenz" in 1,0-1,9 shares in the most
  recent years - Germany's story is a long rise to 2021/22 followed by a plateau, not a continuing climb.

## 6. Other countries

**Canada.** Alberta is the one province with a province-wide external exam, so the school-mark/exam gap is visible:
marks "tend to drop about 10 per cent" when diploma-exam scores are added to the teacher mark, with far larger drops at
some private schools (Calgary Herald investigation, reported by Maclean's); Alberta Education: "in every situation where
you look at the teacher marks and what the exam demonstrates, the teacher marks tend to be higher". The 2013 Auditor
General found Nunavut Grade 12 classroom grades c. 30 points above Alberta exam results. The exam weight was cut from
50 % to 30 % in 2015/16. Ontario has no external exam; Waterloo Engineering's "adjustment factor" (FOI 2018) shows
Ontario high-school admission averages fall on average 16.3 % in first year, with school-specific deductions up to
21.4 points (Ancaster HS, 2022). Volante & DeLuca (Brock): "Educators must commit now to tackle grade inflation".

**Australia.** ATARs rest on moderated school assessment plus external exams; the IB-to-ATAR conversion is a national
weighted average of the NSW, Victoria and SA methods (ACTAC/QTAC paper). The conversion was updated for 2022 entry
(IBA, May 2021), with ACTAC differentiating students on the same IB total so that "not all IB 45s would convert to a
99.95" (secondary source), and a finer-grained IBAS scale from 2023. This is the precedent for the Norwegian problem:
when a coarse 45-point scale is mapped onto a fine-grained national ranking, the top IB score was bunching at the
maximum, and the fix was more resolution within IB, not a lower cap. Research on school assessment vs exam marks in
Australia was not reached (budget).

**Finland.** The matriculation examination is externally graded with the Board setting cut-offs after marking,
targeting c. 5 % laudatur and 15 % eximia - an explicit anti-inflation design. A 2023 study of 6,172 students found exam
grades correlate strongly with three years of course grades. No evidence of grade inflation in Finnish school grades was
found in snippets.

**Netherlands.** Since 2011 the inspectorate measures each school's gap between school-exam (SE) and central-exam (CE)
grades over three years and all subjects; a gap above 0.5 points is "onvoldoende" and the school must act. Parliament
has reported the gap narrowing as SE marks slipped and CE marks rose, but also gaming (schools adding easy components to
the SE to stay under 0.5); the VO-raad reports the inspectorate "voorlopig" not enforcing the indicator.

**France.** Share of mention "très bien" at the bac général: 0.3 % (1967) -> 4.9 % (2006) (Assemblée nationale answer);
13.6 % (2021) -> 14.2 % (2022) (ministry figures, track coverage to verify); in 2024, 68.4 % of general-track candidates
got some mention (DEPP NI 2025-05). The 2021 bac reform moved 40 % of the mark to contrôle continu (school marks) -
**UNVERIFIED (model memory)** - and the jump in TB shares coincides with it; DEPP tables by year are in TO-FETCH.

**Ireland.** The clearest recent case of deliberate deflation. Higher-level H4-or-above share: 62.9 % (2019) -> 77.2 %
(2023 peak) -> 74.2 % (2024); H1 share: 14.3 % (2024) -> 11.7 % (2025) -> 10.1 % (2026) against a pre-pandemic H1 rate
of c. 5-6 %. In 2025 the State cut the post-marking "artificial boost" from an average 7.5 % to 5.5 %, with a multi-year
return to 2019 standards (Minister McEntee); the Irish Times (Aug 2026): "grade inflation down by 1.8 percentage points".

## 7. Cross-national and theoretical points

Only partly covered before the search budget ran out; the items below are leads rather than sourced findings unless a
URL is given.

- Definition used throughout: grade inflation = an increase in awarded grades without a corresponding increase in
  achievement on an external measure (the operational definition in Wikström & Wikström 2005, Hurwitz & Lee 2018 and
  ACT 2022, all of which benchmark grades against tests). Its cost is loss of signal: when 47 % of SAT takers have an
  A-range GPA, the grade no longer discriminates at the top (compression/ceiling), and selective systems respond by
  adding tests (ACT/SAT), adjustment factors (Waterloo), or external exams (Alberta, Netherlands' CE).
- Remedies observed: (i) statistical anchoring of the grade distribution to prior attainment (Ofqual comparable
  outcomes, 2010/11-); (ii) anchoring teacher grades to external tests at school level (Netherlands SE-CE 0.5 rule;
  Sweden's "särskilt beakta" rule, judged ineffective by Riksrevisionen, now being tightened); (iii) externally graded
  exams with post-hoc cut-offs (Finland); (iv) deliberate multi-year deflation (Ireland 2023-, England 2022-23).
- Teacher-assessed vs exam-based grading: the two UK years of teacher assessment raised the A*/A share from 25 % to
  45 % and the GCSE 7+ share from 21 % to 29 %, reverting when exams returned - a direct measure of how much
  teacher-judgement systems drift upward when the external anchor is removed. Alberta (c. 10-point and up to 30-point
  gaps) and the Netherlands (0.5-point threshold) show the same gap in steady state.
- **UNVERIFIED (model memory) leads:** OECD (2012) *Grade Expectations* (PISA 2009) found school marks only loosely
  related to PISA scores and influenced by gender, background and school; Chan, Li & Suen (2007, *International
  Economic Review*) model grade inflation as a signalling equilibrium in which schools inflate to help marginal students
  and employers discount; Diamond & Persson (2016, NBER 22207) document Swedish teachers bumping national-test scores
  over pass thresholds, with lasting effects on pupils' outcomes.

## What the international record shows

1. **Rising school grades with flat or falling external measures is the norm, not a Norwegian anomaly.** US GPA
   2.68 -> 3.11 (1990-2019) and 3.17 -> 3.36 (2010-2021) while SAT and NAEP fell; Danish gymnasium averages rising
   "only one way since 2010" to a record 7.7; Germany's 1,0-1,9 share up from 20 % to 31 % (2006-2024); Sweden's
   meritvärde at a 1998-2021 high while one in six pupils leaves year 9 ineligible; French mentions très bien from
   0.3 % to 14 %. PISA fell in all of these countries over the same period (see the PISA note).
2. **The inflation is concentrated in teacher-set grades and in the top of the scale.** Every country with an
   external benchmark finds teacher grades above it (Sweden +13 % on re-graded essays; Denmark -0.8 exam vs year
   grades; Alberta c. 10 points; Ontario 16 %; England +20 percentage points of A*/A under teacher assessment).
   The top grade is where the compression happens (A* 7.7 % -> 14.3 % in one year; Irish H1 6 % -> 14 %).
3. **Externally examined, criterion-referenced qualifications are the stable reference, and regulators treat them as
   such.** Ofqual anchors on prior attainment; the Netherlands and Sweden anchor on central tests; Finland fixes
   cut-offs after marking. Nobody anchors an external exam to this year's teacher grades. A conversion table that maps
   IB (externally examined, cut-offs set by the IB) onto the Norwegian school-grade distribution therefore inherits
   whatever inflation the Norwegian distribution carries, and will need re-basing every time that distribution moves.
4. **Where IB conversions have been revisited (UK 2016, Australia 2021-23, Sweden 2025, Denmark 2018-), the problems
   were resolution at the top (45 -> 99.95 bunching) and the choice of comparison group (Sweden's natural-science
   programme; Denmark's international vs Danish IB statistics)**, which are exactly the design questions for the
   Norwegian table.
